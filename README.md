# Ebola Is a Business

**What the phrase means, where the money in an epidemic response actually goes, and what good practice looks like.**

ERI Labs | Emergent Reality Intelligence | Jersey City, New Jersey
**29 October 2026**

---

## The short version

In eastern Democratic Republic of the Congo there is a phrase that has followed every Ebola response for the better part of a decade: **Ebola is a business.**

Outsiders usually hear it as a conspiracy theory — people denying the virus is real, or claiming it was invented. That is not what it means, and treating it that way has cost lives.

The most careful study of the phrase, based on 249 people interviewed across Beni, Kayna, Mangina and Mandima during the 2018–20 outbreak, found something more specific and more uncomfortable. People were not rejecting the science. They were describing an economy. Donor money arrives, it is spent by and around a small group, and the people living inside the epidemic watch it pass them by. From that observation they drew a reasonable inference: if the epidemic is the reason the money is here, some of the people spending it have no particular reason to want it over.

The researchers summarised the sentiment in a local saying: *for you the work, for us the money.*

This document takes that claim seriously enough to check it. It maps where money goes in an Ebola response — the 2026 outbreak specifically, and the responses before it — using what is publicly documented. Some of what it finds is damning. Some of it exonerates organisations that get blamed reflexively. Most of it is simply unknown, which is itself the problem.

The headline facts, as of late October 2026:

- **About $962 million in international donor funds has been disbursed** to organisations working on the Ebola response in eastern Congo since the epidemic began, according to Africa CDC's director-general.
- **About $200 million of that has been accounted for** to the Congolese authorities nominally coordinating the response.
- **The Congolese government says it has received no money directly from international donors.** Its communications minister states the funds did not pass through the treasury.
- **Only about a third of implementing organisations** attended a health ministry meeting called to review finances and produced records. Those that did reported receiving $228 million, spending $127 million, and holding roughly $100 million.
- **Congolese authorities have re-accredited 25 of the nearly 100 organisations** previously authorised to operate in the response.
- **Congolese health workers in the response have struck repeatedly over unpaid wages**, beginning in July and recurring since. In Bunia, staff said they had received neither wages nor bonuses since the outbreak was declared in mid-May.

Those six facts, side by side, are the whole argument. Nearly a billion dollars has moved. The people doing the most dangerous work in the epidemic have not reliably been paid. And nobody has published a reconciliation.

That is not proof of theft. **"Unaccounted for" is not the same as "stolen,"** and this document says so repeatedly, because the distinction matters and almost nobody preserves it. But it is proof of something nearly as corrosive: a response that cannot show its own books to the country it is operating in, during an outbreak whose single largest obstacle is that people do not believe what responders tell them.

The last part of this document is the useful part. There is a body of practice — some of it already written into humanitarian commitments, some of it demonstrated by specific organisations in specific outbreaks — that addresses every grievance in that phrase. It is not mysterious. It is pay people on time, publish what you spend, hire locally, disclose your contracts, and leave something behind. The final sections set it out as a checklist, and score the current response against it.

## Part 1: Where the phrase comes from

### The study

The phrase was documented systematically during the 2018–20 Ebola epidemic in North Kivu and Ituri — the tenth outbreak in Congo's history, and until this year the worst.

Researchers conducted 68 semi-structured interviews and 28 focus groups across Beni, Kayna, Mangina and Mandima between July and November 2019, with 249 participants in total: 161 men and 88 women, aged 18 to 80. Participants included Ebola survivors, community members, health workers, responders, local authorities, religious leaders and journalists. Interviews were conducted in French and Swahili.

What they found was not a population that had been misinformed. It was a population that had been watching closely.

### What people actually said

The grievances recorded cluster into five categories, and every one of them is about money or position rather than about virology.

**Vehicles.** Interviewees pointed to the response's fleet — the number of Jeeps, the expense of them. A recurring sardonic reaction when a WHO vehicle went past was to thank the epidemic for it. Journalistic reporting cited in the same work alleged that WHO had rented vehicles from its own employees at inflated prices.

**Hiring.** Local people said they were passed over for jobs. Drivers and other staff were described as being sent from Kinshasa. People with no relevant expertise were appointed, including relatives of officials. The inference drawn locally was nepotism.

**Pay.** This is the sharpest finding, and it is worth setting out as reported:

| Role | Reported daily rate |
|---|---|
| Local doctor (North Kivu) | about $20 |
| Local nurse | about $15 |
| Local cleaner / support staff | about $10 |
| Nurse seconded from Kinshasa | about $90 |
| Doctor seconded from Kinshasa | about $150 |

One doctor said staff in Mangina were earning $4 a day for the same risk. The gap was not experienced as a pay scale. It was experienced as a statement about whose life was worth what.

**Death counts.** Some believed families were paid to declare deaths as Ebola, and that donors rewarded poor performance with more money. The local logic ran the other way: more deaths ought to mean fewer funds, not more. Whether or not the belief is accurate, the reasoning behind it is coherent — people had observed that bad numbers attracted resources.

**Language and conduct.** Responders who spoke neither Swahili nor Kinande, and sensitisers who worked only in Lingala, were read as arrogant outsiders. Some were said to have spoken harshly to patients and families.

### What the phrase does not mean

The study's authors are explicit: this is a rejection of how the response operated, not of the science of Ebola. People were not saying the virus was fictional. They were saying the response was an extraction economy — outside resources captured by a narrow group, a pattern with a name in the political science literature and a long history in the region that long predates any epidemic.

This distinction is the single most practically important thing in this document. The two readings lead to opposite responses:

- If the problem is **misinformation**, the fix is messaging. More radio spots, more sensitisation, more trusted voices repeating that treatment centres save lives.
- If the problem is **an observed economy**, messaging makes it worse. More communication from a source you suspect of profiting reads as a more determined effort to manage you.

Responses have historically chosen the first reading. The evidence points at the second.

### The institutional echo

This is not only a community perception. In May 2019, WHO's own oversight body noted that some of the attacks on responders in North Kivu were linked to frustration over the "Ebola business." The phrase travelled from Beni into the vocabulary of the organisation being described.

## Part 2: The money map of the 2026 outbreak

To ask whether Ebola is a business, you have to be able to follow the money. Here is what can be followed as of 29 October 2026.

### What has been pledged

Pledges are announcements. They are the easiest number to obtain and the least meaningful.

Africa CDC's director-general, Jean Kaseya, has described governments as having **pledged roughly $3 billion**, of which **more than $2 billion remains outstanding**. African countries have committed about **$120 million** between them. The United States has pledged **more than $880 million**, the largest single national commitment.

Separately, Africa CDC and WHO launched a **joint continental preparedness and response plan on 5 June 2026** with a target of **$518 million over six months, June to November**, covering emergency coordination, surveillance, laboratory testing, infection prevention and control, clinical care, community engagement, research, logistics, and support for essential health services — explicitly including maintaining the response to mpox, cholera and measles. That plan's window closes next month. Its stated figure is a fundraising target, not secured money, and no per-pillar costing has been published.

### What has been disbursed

Disbursement is the number that matters, and it is the number that almost nobody publishes.

The one hard figure in circulation comes from Africa CDC, reported on 15 October: **approximately $962 million has been disbursed by international donors directly to organisations implementing the response in eastern Congo.**

The organisations named as working on the ground include WHO, UNICEF, the World Food Programme, Save the Children, Médecins du Monde, and a range of local groups.

### What has been accounted for

**About $200 million.**

That gap — roughly $760 million between what donors say they have released and what implementers have reported to Congolese authorities — is the subject of the current dispute.

The detail is more revealing than the headline. At a health ministry meeting convened to review response finances:

- **Only about one third of invited organisations attended and produced records.**
- Those that did reported **$228 million received**, **$127 million spent**, and **roughly $100 million still held**.

Africa CDC has called the absence of reporting a significant lack of transparency, described it as unacceptable for organisations to receive hundreds of millions without accounting to national authorities, and said it will work with Congolese authorities to trace the remainder. It has not named the organisations that failed to report.

Congolese authorities have responded administratively: the health minister says **25 of nearly 100 previously authorised organisations have been re-accredited**, and implementing organisations must now declare how much they received, from whom, and how they intend to spend it. The communications minister states plainly that the funds did not enter the national treasury.

### What is still needed

Two other figures frame the scale of the shortfall:

- The **United Nations humanitarian appeal for Congo as a whole was 54% funded as of 21 October** — about **$1.15 billion against $2.13 billion**.
- **UNICEF is seeking $150.8 million** for its Ebola response, of which **$113.4 million is for Congo**. About **$40.1 million has been mobilised** — roughly a third.

### Reading the gap honestly

Four possible explanations for the $760 million, and they are not mutually exclusive:

1. **Reporting lag.** Large agencies report to donors on their own cycles, not to host governments on demand. Money spent in July may be formally reported in December.
2. **Different ledgers.** "Disbursed to organisations" counts commitments into accounts. "Spent" counts outlays. A balance held for the next quarter is neither missing nor idle.
3. **Deliberate non-engagement.** Some organisations may be declining to report to a government they do not consider a legitimate or safe counterparty — a position with real precedent in Congo and real justifications, and one that nonetheless produces exactly this optic.
4. **Diversion.** Documented in previous responses. Not demonstrated in this one.

The honest position is that **nobody outside those organisations can currently distinguish between these four**, and that is the finding. A response operating in a region where the central obstacle is the belief that responders are profiting has gone five months without producing a public reconciliation of nearly a billion dollars.

### A timeline of the money

| Date | Event |
|---|---|
| 15 May 2026 | Outbreak declared in Ituri. Genomic work later dates the first infections to around late February |
| 5 June | Africa CDC and WHO launch a joint continental plan with a $518 million target for June–November |
| Early July | First health worker strikes over unpaid wages |
| 15 July | Further strikes as cases pass 2,000 |
| August | Congo receives an allocation of 70,000 Ervebo doses — 50,000 for frontline workers, 20,000 for research |
| 6 August | Health workers protest at the governor's office in Bunia, saying they have been unpaid since mid-May |
| September | International pledging meeting; Africa CDC subsequently describes roughly $2.9 billion in associated commitments |
| 15 October | Africa CDC states $962 million disbursed, about $200 million accounted for |
| 19 October | Ministry finance meeting reported: about a third of organisations attend; those that do report $228 million received, $127 million spent |
| Late October | Congo re-accredits 25 of nearly 100 organisations; implementers required to declare receipts and spending plans |
| 21 October | UN humanitarian appeal for Congo stands at 54% funded |
| 23 October | Tumaini vaccine trial begins in Bunia |
| 26 October | Regeneron extends its US stockpile contract for Inmazeb by about $270 million through 2031 |
| 27 October | Confirmed cases pass 10,000 |

Read down that column and the shape of the problem is visible without any interpretation. The money arrives, the plans are launched, the trials begin — and the line about unpaid wages recurs from July to the present without resolution.

## Part 3: The three tiers of the Ebola economy

Money in an epidemic response moves through three distinct layers. They have different economics, different accountability mechanisms, and different failure modes. Conflating them is how most public argument about this goes wrong.

### Tier one: global capital and procurement

This is where the largest sums sit, and where the least of it ever touches the outbreak zone.

It includes sovereign pledges, multilateral financing instruments, vaccine stockpile funding, and government procurement contracts with pharmaceutical manufacturers. Decisions are made in Washington, Geneva, Brussels and Oslo. Accountability is to legislatures, boards and shareholders.

The defining feature of tier one is that **money can be committed here without any of it moving toward a patient**. Part 7 examines this layer in detail, because it contains the clearest documented case of an Ebola financing mechanism that paid investors more reliably than it paid countries.

### Tier two: international implementers

UN agencies, international NGOs, contractors and the bilateral agencies that fund them. This is where the $962 million went.

The economics here are not sinister but they are expensive by design. International staff carry international salaries, insurance, security and evacuation costs. Specialist logistics — cold chain to -80°C, biosafe laboratories, protective equipment at scale — are genuinely costly. A response that can airlift a laboratory into a conflict zone in a week is purchasing speed, and speed costs.

The failure mode in tier two is not usually theft. It is **opacity plus overhead**: a structure where a dollar's journey from donor to delivery passes through several organisations, each taking a legitimate cut, with no published trace of the path. By the time the dollar arrives in Beni it may be forty cents, all of it lawfully spent, and nobody can show the arithmetic to the person who watched the Land Cruiser go past.

### Tier three: the local response economy

This is the tier that generates the phrase.

In an outbreak zone, the response becomes the largest employer, the largest renter of property and vehicles, and the largest consumer of fuel, food and services, often within weeks. It creates:

- **Jobs** — hygienists, burial team members, contact tracers, drivers, cooks, security guards, community mobilisers — usually paid as daily workers rather than staff.
- **Rents** — vehicles, houses, offices, warehouses, at prices well above the local market.
- **Per diems** — daily allowances which, for many workers, exceed their actual salaries.
- **A hiring gate** — and therefore a position of enormous power for whoever controls it.

None of this is corrupt by nature. All of it is distorting by nature. A response that injects tens of millions of dollars into a district economy with no pre-existing payroll infrastructure, under time pressure, in a conflict zone, will produce rent-seeking unless it is designed specifically not to.

**The 2018–20 response was not designed not to.** Part 4 sets out what was documented.

## Part 4: The receipts — what is actually on the record

This section deals only with documented findings: internal reviews, investigations, court outcomes, published audits and reported records. Where something is an allegation that was denied, it is labelled as such.

### Vehicle rentals, 2018–20

WHO's fleet in the response was about **700 rented vehicles**, costing between **$1.3 million and $1.9 million per month** from March to September 2019.

A WHO internal review in June 2019 found a mismatch between the number of leased vehicles, their cost and the actual workload, and found no standard operating procedures for logistics.

Reporting identified around a dozen civil servants in Butembo renting private cars to the response at **$1,800–$3,000 per vehicle per month** — about half of them also worked for the response. Two individuals were named in that reporting, one said to have earned nearly $10,000 from a rental and another about $100,000 from seven vehicles between December 2018 and June 2019. **Both denied it.** WHO said rental contracts had shifted to local agencies by February 2019, though documents suggested direct rentals continued until July, and WHO opened an internal investigation after the reporting appeared.

This single item is the material basis of that sardonic reaction. People in Butembo could see the cars, knew who owned several of them, and could do the arithmetic.

### "Opération retour"

WHO paid a **$10–15 daily top-up** to daily labourers working under the Ministry of Health, on top of government salaries.

Workers described handing part of that money back to whoever had hired them. One said he was asked for at least **30% of his monthly pay**. The practice had a name locally: *opération retour*.

This is the mechanism that converts a hiring gate into an income stream. It does not require anyone at headquarters to steal anything. It requires only that the person who decides who works takes a cut from those who want to.

### Duplicate payrolls

Leaked response payroll data showed **at least 60 people listed in multiple full-time response jobs** as of August 2019. WHO said it could not verify the figures without seeing the underlying material, and noted it was working with an external firm to reconcile payment records and building a **biometric payment database** — which is, as Part 9 argues, exactly the right fix.

### Security payments

WHO paid **1,386 Congolese security personnel $598,800 for June 2019 alone** — roughly **$300 per person per month**, against a UN standard police per diem of $3–4 per day. WHO said the rate was set by the Congolese government and that the payments have since stopped. Its donor reporting listed **$5.4 million on security between August 2018 and June 2019 with no breakdown**, and it acknowledged that full UN human rights due diligence on the security forces involved had not been completed at the start of the response.

### Suppression and the Mouzoko killing

A draft operational review, based on more than 400 interviews across the aid sector in Congo, reported that attempts to raise abuses were stifled. It cited the **April 2019 killing of WHO epidemiologist Dr Richard Valery Mouzoko** in Butembo; several doctors were arrested in connection and later released. WHO would not say whether Mouzoko had raised concerns. An adviser quoted in the same reporting said permanent staff who raised alarms were sent home.

Nothing in the public record establishes a link between the killing and the financial allegations. It belongs here because the review raised it, and because a response in which whistleblowing is reportedly unsafe cannot be audited from the inside.

### The minister

Congo's former health minister, **Oly Ilunga, was arrested in September 2019 and subsequently jailed for diverting response funds.** He disputes the case against him.

### West Africa, 2014–16

The pattern is not specific to Congo. The International Federation of Red Cross and Red Crescent Societies disclosed that it **lost more than $5 million to fraud in the West Africa response**, with auditors finding overpriced supplies and payments to workers who did not exist. IFRC has since said it strengthened its fraud and corruption prevention.

Two features of that disclosure are worth noting, because they cut in opposite directions. The first is that an organisation with a century of field experience, operating in three countries with functioning governments and heavy international scrutiny, still lost millions to the same two mechanisms documented later in Congo: inflated supply prices and payroll padding. These are not exotic failures. They are what happens by default when a large payroll and a large procurement operation are built in weeks.

The second is that the loss became public **because the organisation disclosed it and said what it had changed**. That is the difference between an institution with an audit culture and one without, and it is the standard against which the current silence over $760 million should be judged. The scandal is not that money goes astray in emergencies; it is that in this response nobody has yet published the arithmetic that would show whether it has.

### The appropriation that sat still

The United States appropriated **$5.4 billion** in emergency Ebola funding for FY2015 — **$3.74 billion for international response, $1.15 billion for domestic response, and $515 million for research and development**.

A year later, published tracking put **total international obligations at roughly $2.03 billion**, with approximately **$1.3 billion still unobligated at USAID**, and noted that agency expenditure reports required by Congress were not public.

That is not fraud. It is the other failure mode, and in some ways the more important one: money appropriated at the peak of attention, moving far more slowly than the epidemic it was appropriated for, with the spending record unavailable to the public that provided it.

### What the record supports, and what it does not

Taken together, the documented record supports four conclusions:

1. **Rent extraction around hiring and asset rental is real, recurrent, and structural.** It has been found in two separate major responses by two separate mechanisms.
2. **The amounts involved are small relative to total spend and enormous relative to local incomes.** A $100,000 vehicle-rental income is a rounding error against $962 million and a fortune in Butembo. Both facts drive the politics.
3. **Most of the money is not stolen.** The dominant pattern is opacity and slowness, not theft.
4. **Opacity and theft are indistinguishable from the outside** — which is why, for the purposes of an epidemic that runs on trust, they have nearly the same effect.

## Part 5: The pay gap

If there is a single number at the centre of *Ebola is a business*, it is not the $962 million. It is the daily rate.

### Then

The 2019 figures reported above are stark enough to restate: a local doctor at roughly $20 a day, a nurse at $15, a cleaner at $10, against a Kinshasa-seconded nurse at about $90 and a Kinshasa doctor at about $150 — for work described as less risky. In Mangina, $4 a day was reported for the same exposure.

### Now

In the 2026 outbreak, the complaint has shifted from *too little* to *not at all*.

- Health workers began striking over pay in **early July 2026**, with actions recurring through July and August.
- In **Bunia**, the capital of Ituri — the province that has carried roughly three-quarters of all cases — workers protesting at the provincial governor's office in early August said they had received **neither wages nor bonuses since the outbreak was declared in mid-May**.
- Workers in Bunia have described going two months without pay, with outstanding payments owed for the first 45 days of the response and unpaid transport allowances covering a 75-day period.
- Strikes and protests have continued into October. A nurse in Bunia, asked about the $962 million figure, said many people working in the response had not been paid and called that unacceptable.
- Congolese authorities have said repeatedly that payments are being processed, attributing delays to the administrative work of verifying which workers were actually deployed and ensuring money reaches the right people.
- At least **50 health workers have died** of Ebola in this outbreak.

### Why this is the whole argument

Consider what a person in Beni or Bunia can observe directly, without access to any ledger:

- International organisations are present in force, with vehicles, equipment and expatriate staff.
- A figure approaching a billion dollars has been reported in the press.
- Their neighbour, a nurse who goes into a treatment centre every day, has not been paid since May.

No conspiracy theory is required to get from those three observations to *Ebola is a business*. It is the most parsimonious available explanation. Any accurate alternative explanation — reporting lags, parallel payment channels, verification requirements, the distinction between a UN agency's budget and a government payroll — is more complicated, less visible, and delivered by the party under suspicion.

**This is why unpaid wages are not an HR problem in an Ebola response. They are an epidemiological problem.** They are the most legible possible evidence for the belief that keeps people out of treatment centres, and clearing them costs a fraction of one percent of what has already been disbursed.

## Part 6: The per diem problem

Daily allowances deserve their own section, because they are the single most distorting instrument in humanitarian response finance and almost nobody outside the sector knows they exist.

### How it works

In an emergency response, large numbers of people are engaged not as employees but as **daily workers**, paid a per diem — a daily allowance notionally covering transport, meals and incidental costs. The rate is set by the paying agency, sometimes in negotiation with the host government.

In a context where a government nurse's official monthly salary may be a fraction of what the response pays per week, the per diem is not a cost reimbursement. **It is the actual income.**

### What that produces

**A hiring market.** When a daily allowance exceeds a monthly salary, the ability to put a name on a list becomes a tradeable asset. *Opération retour* is what that market looks like when it clears.

**Ghost workers.** Payrolls built at speed, in several languages, across dozens of health zones, with cash payments and no biometric verification, produce duplicate and fictitious entries. Sixty people in multiple full-time jobs, in one leaked dataset, is the documented version.

**Salary inversion.** Staff abandon routine health services — vaccination, maternal care, malaria — for the better-paid Ebola response. This is a well-known mechanism for the collapse of routine care during outbreaks, and it has a measurable cost: in Mongbwalu, the epicentre town of this outbreak, first-dose measles coverage among children fell by 69% during the response.

**A cliff.** When the outbreak ends, the per diems stop, overnight, for thousands of people. The health system is then poorer in trained staff and public goodwill than before. The next outbreak begins from there.

**Strikes.** Because per diems are the income, a delay is not an inconvenience; it is destitution. Which is why pay disputes in Ebola responses do not simmer — they stop the response.

### The uncomfortable part

Per diems exist because they work. They allow an agency to mobilise 5,000 people in a fortnight without creating payroll obligations it cannot sustain, in countries where civil service pay systems may be months in arrears. The alternative — building a real payroll — is slower precisely when speed saves lives.

The practice question is therefore not whether to use daily payments but **how to stop them functioning as an unaudited parallel economy**: published rates, biometric verification, direct payment to the worker, no intermediary with discretion over who is listed, and a plan on day one for what happens when they end. Every one of those is achievable. Part 9 treats them as the core of best practice.

## Part 7: The global tier — bonds, stockpiles and contracts

The local economy is where the phrase is spoken. The global tier is where the largest sums move, and it contains the most literal example of Ebola as a business.

### The pandemic bonds

In June 2017 the World Bank launched the **Pandemic Emergency Financing Facility (PEF)**, an insurance mechanism designed to release money to poor countries fast when an outbreak crossed defined thresholds.

Its structure:

- An **insurance window of up to $425 million** in potential payouts, funded by **$320 million of catastrophe bonds** in two tranches ($225 million Class A, $95 million Class B) plus **$105 million in swaps**.
- A smaller **cash window of up to $64 million**.
- Around **$190 million in donor contributions** from three countries and the World Bank's concessional arm.
- Investors were paid annual coupons of **6.5% over Libor** on Class A and **11.1% over Libor** on Class B, with Germany and Japan funding the interest.

Then the 2018–20 Ebola epidemic happened in eastern Congo, killing more than 2,000 people.

**The bonds did not pay out.** The Class B trigger required, among other conditions, at least 20 deaths in a second country — a condition rarely met by Ebola outbreaks, which tend to stay in one country while killing thousands inside it. Investors lost nothing. Roughly **$75.5 million in premiums was paid to bondholders during that epidemic.** Congo received **$31 million** from the facility, and it came from the donor-funded cash window, not the bonds.

PEF eventually triggered for COVID-19, paying out close to **$196 million** on 17 April 2020 — nearly four months after the outbreak was first identified, and after a mandatory 12-week waiting period that critics argued had squandered the only phase in which the money would have been decisive.

Lawrence Summers, a former World Bank chief economist, called the facility **"an embarrassing mistake."** Public health scholars argued throughout that the premiums would have bought more protection if spent directly on surveillance, diagnostics and response capacity.

Two readings are available. The defenders' case is real: the facility was an attempt to pre-commit money before the political attention cycle began, and pre-commitment in disaster financing almost always costs a premium. The critics' case is that an instrument which paid investors tens of millions during an epidemic that killed thousands, and delivered its own beneficiaries nothing from the insured portion, is a textbook illustration of the phrase this document is named after.

Whichever reading is right, **this is the strongest-form version of "Ebola is a business," and it did not come from Beni. It came from Washington.**

### The vaccine stockpile

The global Ebola vaccine stockpile is the counter-example, and a good one.

- Funded by **Gavi**, which authorised **$178 million** to build it following licensure of Merck's rVSV-ZEBOV vaccine.
- Targeted at **500,000 doses**, a level recommended by WHO's immunisation advisory group.
- **Managed by UNICEF**, physically stored in Switzerland, shippable within seven days in ultra-cold (-80°C) packaging.
- **Allocated by the International Coordinating Group** on Vaccine Provision, whose members are WHO, UNICEF, the IFRC and MSF.
- **Free of charge to Gavi-eligible low- and lower-middle-income countries**, which also receive operational support for delivery.
- Any country reporting cases can apply.

That is a well-designed public good: pooled financing, neutral allocation, free at the point of need, operationally pre-positioned. It is the mechanism that put **70,000 doses of Ervebo into Congo in August 2026** — 50,000 for frontline and health workers, 20,000 for research.

Its limitation in this outbreak is biological rather than financial. The stockpile holds a vaccine licensed against the Zaire species. **This outbreak is Bundibugyo**, and whether Ervebo protects against it is the question the Tumaini trial, launched in Bunia on 23 October, exists to answer.

### Procurement

Three contracts from this year show how the commercial tier works, and they are not all the same story.

**Regeneron and BARDA.** On 26 October 2026, Regeneron extended its 2020 agreement with the US Biomedical Advanced Research and Development Authority to supply additional doses of **Inmazeb** through 2031, for approximately **$270 million**. Inmazeb is licensed against the **Zaire** species. It is a US stockpiling decision for future outbreaks, not a purchase of treatment for the epidemic now running. Separately — and this is the part that matters for Congo — Regeneron is putting **maftivimab**, the most potent neutralising antibody in that cocktail, into the **PARTNERS** trial as a candidate treatment for Bundibugyo, on the strength of laboratory and non-human-primate data.

**The Serum Institute of India** has manufactured **620,000 doses** of the Oxford Bundibugyo vaccine candidate at its own risk, before knowing whether it works.

**Moderna** has committed at least **500,000 doses** of its Bundibugyo candidate to lower-income countries at access pricing if it is licensed.

**CEPI** funds the Bundibugyo vaccine programme that pays for the Tumaini trial, backed by the European Commission, the US State Department, Portugal's science foundation, the Republic of Korea and CEPI's own core funding.

Those four facts complicate any simple reading of the pharmaceutical tier. Manufacturing 620,000 doses of an unproven vaccine at your own expense is not profiteering; it is the opposite bet. The honest summary is that **the commercial tier behaves differently depending on whether a licensed product exists.** Where one does — Inmazeb, Ervebo — there are stockpile contracts, procurement cycles and a market. Where none does, as with Bundibugyo, the money has to come from public and philanthropic funders, and it largely has.

## Part 8: Why this is epidemiology, not ethics

It would be possible to read everything above as a story about integrity, and to file it alongside other arguments about aid effectiveness. That would be a mistake. In an Ebola outbreak, the perception of the response's finances is a **transmission variable**.

The chain is short and every link is measurable.

**Link one: people believe the response profits from the epidemic.** In October 2026, a survey by the International Rescue Committee found that **85% of respondents in North Kivu said distrust of community responders was keeping them from seeking care.** Responders report fighting the belief that teams are there to make money, and the related question of why teams arrive for Ebola but not for malaria or typhoid.

**Link two: people therefore do not come forward.** Roughly **64% of deaths in this outbreak are occurring in the community rather than in treatment centres** — a figure that has been near-constant for months despite enormous increases in capacity. Congo now has roughly **2,000 treatment beds across more than 50 centres** and about two dozen diagnostic laboratories. The beds are not the binding constraint.

**Link three: the response cannot see the epidemic.** About **80% of new confirmed cases arise outside the chains of transmission being monitored.** Africa CDC's threshold for an outbreak considered under control is the reverse: at least **95% of new cases should come from people already on a contact list**. National contact follow-up stands at **79.7%** against a required 95%, and at **73.3%** in North Kivu.

**Link four: deaths at home generate more infections.** Families care for the dying and prepare the dead, and a body is at its most infectious just after death. Community deaths are therefore not merely a measure of failure; they are a driver of the next generation of cases.

The loop closes. Mistrust produces community deaths, community deaths produce more infections, more infections confirm that the centres are not working, and the belief that the response is a business is reinforced by the only evidence most people have: their own neighbourhood.

**This is why the accounting dispute is an outbreak-control issue.** A published reconciliation of $962 million would not cure anything by itself. But it addresses the one belief that sits upstream of every other failure in the chain, and it is within the gift of the organisations holding the money.

## Part 9: Best practices

Everything above is diagnosis. This is the part that can be acted on.

These practices are drawn from what has been documented to go wrong, what specific organisations have already demonstrated works, and what the sector has formally committed to and not delivered. Each is stated as an action, with the evidence behind it and a test for whether it is actually being done.

---

### A. Pay

#### 1. Pay frontline workers first, before anything else is procured

**Why.** Unpaid wages are the most visible possible confirmation of *Ebola is a business*, and strikes stop the response outright. In this outbreak, health workers have been striking since July while $962 million moved.

**What it means in practice.** Treat frontline compensation as a protected budget line that cannot be reprogrammed, funded at the start of the response rather than from whatever remains after logistics.

**Test.** Can the response state, publicly, the date through which every frontline worker has been paid? If not, it is not doing this.

#### 2. Publish the pay scale, in local languages, at every site

**Why.** The 2019 pay gap — $20 a day locally against $90 or $150 for seconded staff — did its damage partly because it was discovered rather than disclosed. A published scale converts a scandal into a negotiation.

**What it means in practice.** Every role, every rate, every allowance, posted at the treatment centre and the health zone office, in French and Swahili or Kinande as appropriate.

**Test.** Can a hygienist read, on a wall, what a doctor at the same site earns?

#### 3. Close the differential between local and seconded staff, and justify what remains

**Why.** Some differential is defensible — relocation, hardship, specialist scarcity. A sevenfold differential for identical work in the same ward is not, and is read as a statement about whose risk counts.

**What it means in practice.** A single risk-based hazard component paid identically to everyone exposed, with location-based supplements declared separately and capped.

**Test.** Does the hazard payment for entering a high-risk zone depend on where the worker was hired?

#### 4. Pay directly to the worker, with biometric or equivalent verification

**Why.** This is the specific fix for *opération retour* and for duplicate payrolls. If a hiring intermediary cannot touch the money, they cannot take a share of it; if payment requires a biometric match, one person cannot hold four jobs. WHO was building exactly such a database after 2019, and the approach is correct.

**What it means in practice.** Mobile money or bank transfer direct to the individual, biometric enrolment at registration, a single deduplicated response payroll across all agencies in a health zone.

**Test.** Does any individual have discretion over whose name appears on a payment list without an audit trail?

#### 5. Never let a per diem silently become a salary

**Why.** Per diems that exceed real salaries create a hiring market, strip routine services of staff, and end in a cliff when the outbreak does.

**What it means in practice.** Daily rates set in reference to local salary scales and published as such; a declared transition plan from day one for what happens when the response winds down; a share of response funding ring-fenced for retaining trained staff in routine services afterwards.

**Test.** Is there a written answer to "what happens to these 5,000 people when this ends?" — and was it written at the start rather than at the end?

---

### B. Publish

#### 6. Publish disbursements, not pledges

**Why.** Pledges are announcements; they make headlines and move nothing. The gap between roughly $3 billion pledged and $962 million disbursed, and between $962 million disbursed and $200 million accounted for, is where the credibility of the entire response currently sits.

**What it means in practice.** A single public ledger per outbreak: donor, recipient organisation, amount committed, amount transferred, amount spent, date. Updated monthly.

**Test.** Can a journalist in Bunia find out how much money a named organisation received for the health zone she lives in?

#### 7. Report to the host country, not only to the donor

**Why.** Every organisation in this response reports to someone. Almost none of them report to Congo. When a government states publicly that it has received no funds and does not know who holds them, the resulting vacuum is filled by the most plausible local theory.

**What it means in practice.** A standing requirement, written into grant agreements by donors, that implementers file the same financial reports with the national coordination structure that they file with their funders.

**Test.** Did a third of invited organisations fail to show up to a ministry finance meeting? In this response, yes.

#### 8. Publish your own accounts without being asked

**Why.** It is possible, and it has been done. MSF published its Ebola financial accounting during the West Africa epidemic: **€70 million raised from private donors and €15 million in public funds in 2014, about €60 million spent on the response by year end**, nearly all of it in Liberia, Sierra Leone and Guinea, with roughly €48 million planned for the following year. That is a transparency standard set in the middle of the worst outbreak in history by an organisation with no obligation to meet it.

**What it means in practice.** Annual or outbreak-specific financial accounting, published, in a form a non-specialist can read.

**Test.** Is the organisation's outbreak spending discoverable by someone who is not a donor?

#### 9. Disclose asset rentals and the people behind them

**Why.** The vehicle-rental findings of 2019 — about 700 vehicles at $1.3–1.9 million a month, with civil servants who also worked for the response renting cars at $1,800–$3,000 each — are the single most legible local grievance in the record. They were discovered by journalists, not disclosed by anyone.

**What it means in practice.** A public register of leased vehicles, buildings and equipment: asset, monthly price, counterparty, and a declared-interest field. A standing rule that no one employed by the response may rent assets to it.

**Test.** Does a conflict-of-interest register exist for this response, and is it public?

#### 10. Publish security payments in full

**Why.** $5.4 million on security over eleven months with no breakdown, and $598,800 to 1,386 personnel in a single month at roughly a hundred times the applicable UN per diem, is a line item that invites exactly the interpretation it received.

**What it means in practice.** Full disclosure of payments to security forces, the rate basis, who set it, and completed human rights due diligence before any such arrangement begins.

**Test.** Was due diligence completed before the first payment, or acknowledged as incomplete afterwards?

---

### C. Hire and localise

#### 11. Hire locally by default, and publish the ratio

**Why.** "Drivers were sent from Kinshasa" is not a complaint about logistics. It is a complaint about a response treating a region as a worksite rather than a community. Local hiring also buys language, geography and standing that no amount of sensitisation can purchase.

**What it means in practice.** A published target for the share of response staff recruited within the affected province, reported monthly by role level — not just the share of hygienists, but the share of coordinators.

**Test.** What proportion of decision-making roles in this response are held by people from Ituri and North Kivu?

#### 12. Make recruitment contestable

**Why.** Nepotism allegations in 2019 — appointments of people without relevant expertise, including relatives of officials — are a predictable product of discretionary hiring at speed with no appeal.

**What it means in practice.** Posted vacancies, published selection criteria, a complaints route that does not run through the person who made the appointment.

**Test.** Can a rejected candidate find out why?

#### 13. Fund local and national organisations directly

**Why.** The sector committed to this and has not done it. The Grand Bargain's 2016 target was **25% of humanitarian funding to local and national actors, as directly as possible**. The measured reality among signatories: **0.5% direct in 2022 and 0.6% in 2023 — about $174 million.** Over the same period total signatory funding rose from $20 billion to $27.8 billion while direct funding to local actors rose by $74 million. Across all donors, direct allocations to local and national actors reached **$1.7 billion in 2023, 4.5% of trackable funding** — and much of that increase came from two Gulf donors funding specific crises rather than any shift in practice. The only figure that reaches 25% is self-reported by intermediaries and cannot be independently verified.

**What it means in practice.** Direct grants to Congolese organisations, with overheads attached rather than stripped, and published as a share of total response spending.

**Test.** What share of the $962 million went directly to Congolese organisations? Nobody can currently answer this, which is the finding.

---

### D. Listen and protect

#### 14. Build a complaints mechanism with teeth, and resource it like a pillar

**Why.** A draft sector-wide review based on more than 400 interviews found that attempts to report abuses were stifled, and that staff who raised alarms were reportedly removed. A response that cannot be criticised from within cannot be corrected from within.

**What it means in practice.** An independent reporting channel, available in local languages, with published case numbers and outcomes, run by someone outside the response hierarchy.

**Test.** How many complaints were received last month, and how many were resolved?

#### 15. Treat sexual exploitation as a financial-control failure as well as a protection failure

**Why.** Allegations that sex was demanded as a condition of employment or pay sit in the same structural place as *opération retour*: both are products of one person holding unaccountable discretion over another person's income. The 2018–20 response produced a documented abuse scandal that North Kivu has not forgotten, and which is cited in this outbreak as a reason not to cooperate.

**What it means in practice.** Every control that removes discretion over hiring and payment — biometric payroll, posted vacancies, direct transfer — is also a safeguarding control. They should be designed and audited together.

**Test.** Does the safeguarding plan reference the payroll system?

#### 16. Never use armed enforcement for public health

**Why.** In August 2026, authorities in Butembo created a task force drawn from pro-government militia fighters to protect health workers and facilities; by late September armed men were operating handwashing checkpoints on roads into the city. Both local civil society and WHO flagged the risk at the time. The same conversion of a trust problem into a security problem in 2018–20 is part of why North Kivu is the hardest place in this outbreak.

**What it means in practice.** Protection of facilities and staff where necessary; never coercion of patients, contacts or families.

**Test.** Is anyone being compelled, at a checkpoint or otherwise, to comply with a health measure?

---

### E. Leave something behind

#### 17. Spend on the health system, not only on the outbreak

**Why.** The strongest version of the local critique is not that money was stolen. It is that teams arrive for Ebola and not for malaria or typhoid — the diseases that kill people there every year. Responders in this outbreak report hearing exactly that. The pattern is visible in the data: in Mongbwalu, first-dose measles coverage fell 69% during the response.

**What it means in practice.** A fixed share of every outbreak budget spent on routine services in the same health zones, during the outbreak, and continuing after it.

**Test.** Did routine immunisation coverage in affected health zones rise or fall during the response?

#### 18. Buy and build things that stay

**Why.** A rented vehicle fleet at $1.3–1.9 million a month leaves nothing when the contract ends. A laboratory leaves capacity. The best public health investment of this outbreak is a case in point: the multiplex test authorised in September 2026 detects all four human Ebola species plus Marburg, Crimean-Congo haemorrhagic fever and Lassa, **and runs on machines already installed across central and east Africa**. Existing equipment went from useless to usable without buying new instruments.

**What it means in practice.** Prefer purchase to rental where the asset has post-outbreak value; prefer platforms the country already owns; hand over equipment on exit with a maintenance budget.

**Test.** What will physically remain in Ituri and North Kivu in June 2027?

#### 19. Plan the exit in the first month

**Why.** The end of an outbreak is the end of thousands of incomes and the beginning of the next trust deficit. Responses that leave abruptly teach the lesson that generates *Ebola is a business* in the first place: they came, they spent, they left.

**What it means in practice.** A published transition plan at the start, with named funding for the twelve months after the last case.

**Test.** Does one exist?

#### 20. Fund preparedness at a level that makes the panic cycle unnecessary

**Why.** This is the structural argument behind all of the above. The 2015 US appropriation of $5.4 billion came after the West Africa epidemic had already killed more than 11,000 people, and a year later roughly $1.3 billion of it was still unobligated at a single agency. The pandemic bond premiums — about $75.5 million paid to investors during the Kivu epidemic — would have bought a great deal of surveillance. Money that arrives at the peak of attention is expensive, slow and distorting. Money spent before an outbreak is cheap, fast and invisible.

**What it means in practice.** Sustained financing of routine surveillance, laboratory networks and health worker salaries between outbreaks, judged against the cost of the emergency it avoids.

**Test.** What was spent on Ebola preparedness in Ituri in 2025?

### F. Three further practices that are cheap and almost never done

#### 21. Measure trust and publish the measurement

**Why.** Case counts, bed numbers and doses delivered are all tracked daily. The variable that actually governs whether the response works — whether people believe it — is measured occasionally, by outside organisations, and published late. The one recent measurement, finding 85% of respondents in North Kivu kept from care by distrust of responders, came from an NGO survey rather than from the response's own reporting.

**What it means in practice.** A standing, published trust and care-seeking indicator per health zone, reported on the same cadence as case counts.

**Test.** Is there a trust figure in the daily situation report?

#### 22. Pay survivors, and pay the families of dead health workers

**Why.** Survivors are the strongest possible counter-evidence to the belief that treatment centres kill people, and they are routinely stigmatised and left without income. Fifty health workers have died in this outbreak. What happens to their households is both a moral question and a recruitment question for the next outbreak.

**What it means in practice.** A published survivor support package — income, follow-up care, public recognition — and a death-in-service payment for frontline workers that is written down before anyone dies.

**Test.** Does a named, funded survivor and bereavement scheme exist?

#### 23. Buy local where buying local is possible

**Why.** Every dollar of food, fuel, construction, laundry and transport procured from a local supplier is a dollar that argues against the extraction reading. Every dollar flown in argues for it.

**What it means in practice.** A published local-procurement share, with a target, reported alongside spending.

**Test.** What share of non-medical procurement in Ituri and North Kivu was bought in Ituri and North Kivu?

## Part 11: What good looks like

It is easy to write a document like this that reads as though nothing works. Several things demonstrably do, and they are worth naming precisely because they show the standard is achievable.

**The vaccine stockpile.** Pooled financing of $178 million, a 500,000-dose target, neutral allocation by a four-agency group, free doses for eligible countries, and shipping within seven days at -80°C. When Congo requested doses in August 2026, 70,000 were allocated. This is what a well-designed global public good looks like, and nobody in Beni has to take it on faith.

**Published accounts.** MSF's financial accounting during the West Africa epidemic — funds raised, funds spent, where — set a standard in the middle of the worst outbreak on record. Any organisation holding part of the $962 million could do the same this month.

**Testing that uses what already exists.** The multiplex panel authorised in September 2026 covers all four human Ebola species plus three other haemorrhagic fevers and runs on machines already installed across the region. It converted the region's existing laboratory estate from useless for this outbreak to usable, without new instruments. It is the clearest example in this outbreak of money spent on capacity rather than on presence.

**Manufacturing at risk.** The Serum Institute has made 620,000 doses of an unproven Bundibugyo vaccine before knowing whether it works, and Moderna has committed 500,000 doses at access pricing if licensed. Neither is a profit-maximising decision.

**Transparent daily reporting.** Kenya's health ministry published case-by-case updates throughout its October cluster — nine cases, two deaths, 135 contacts traced and quarantined, and an explicit public request that released contacts be welcomed home because they pose no risk. Nine cases is a small problem compared with 10,000, but the reporting standard is replicable at any scale.

**Biometric payroll.** It was the right answer in 2019 and it remains the right answer. It is a solved engineering problem being treated as an open policy question.

If this document had to be compressed into a dashboard, these are the entries. Each is public, falsifiable, and currently either moving or conspicuously stuck.

2. **Organisations reporting.** A third attended the ministry finance meeting; 25 of ~100 re-accredited. Watch both numbers.

## Part 13: Questions people actually ask

**Is the money being stolen?**

Some of it has been, in previous responses, by identifiable mechanisms: kickbacks on daily labour pay, asset rentals by officials who also worked for the response, padded payrolls, and in one case a health minister convicted of diverting funds. Whether any of it is being stolen in this response is not publicly known. The $760 million gap between disbursed and accounted for is a reporting gap, and four ordinary explanations fit it before theft is required.

**Then why does everyone in Beni think it is?**

Because the four ordinary explanations are invisible and the Land Cruisers are not. People are reasoning from what they can see: a large, well-equipped foreign presence, a reported figure approaching a billion dollars, and a neighbour who has not been paid since May. That is not a failure of reasoning.

**Are the big agencies profiting?**

Not in the sense usually meant. The documented diversion sits at the level of individuals and intermediaries, not institutions. But international response is genuinely expensive — international salaries, security, insurance, cold chain, airlift — and a structure in which a dollar passes through several organisations before it reaches a health zone will deliver less than a dollar, entirely lawfully. The problem is that nobody publishes the fraction, so the public is left to guess, and it guesses low.

**Why not just pay the nurses?**

This is the right question. The obstacles given publicly are administrative: verifying which workers were actually deployed, in which health zone, under whose payroll, and ensuring money reaches the right individual rather than an intermediary. Those are real problems. They are also solved problems — biometric enrolment and direct transfer — and they have been the recommended fix since 2019.

**Is Ebola worth this much money when malaria kills more people here every year?**

Responders in this outbreak report being asked exactly this, and there is no comfortable answer. Ebola attracts emergency financing because it is frightening, fast and internationally transmissible; malaria does not, because it is none of those things. The practical response is not to spend less on Ebola but to stop the Ebola response cannibalising routine services, which is measurable: first-dose measles coverage fell 69% in the epicentre town during this response.

**Does any of this change how many people die?**

Yes, and that is the entire argument. About 64% of deaths are occurring at home rather than in treatment centres, roughly 80% of new cases arise outside the contact lists being monitored, and 85% of surveyed people in the worst-affected province say distrust of responders keeps them from care. A body is at its most infectious just after death, and families care for the dying. The belief that the response is a business is upstream of every one of those numbers.

**What would actually fix it?**

Pay the arrears, publish the ledger, and hire locally. All three are within the gift of organisations that already hold the money, all three cost a fraction of one percent of what has been disbursed, and all three have been recommended since the last outbreak in the same province.

## Part 14: What this document does not claim

Precision about the limits of the evidence is the price of making the argument at all.

- **It does not claim that $760 million has been stolen.** It claims that $760 million has not been publicly reconciled, that four explanations are available, and that no one outside the holding organisations can currently distinguish between them.
- **It does not claim that aid organisations are profiting from the epidemic.** The documented diversion in previous responses involved local hiring intermediaries, asset rentals by individuals, inflated security payments and one convicted minister. That is a different allegation from institutional profiteering, and the record supports the first rather than the second.
- **It does not treat denied allegations as findings.** The individuals named in the 2019 vehicle-rental reporting denied the allegations, and that denial is recorded here alongside the claim.
- **It does not know what the current response's pay scales, hiring ratios, asset registers or security payments are.** Eleven of twenty best practices are scored unknown because the information is not public, not because it is known to be bad.
- **It does not claim the pandemic bond facility was fraudulent.** It was a disclosed instrument whose terms were public. The criticism is of its design and its results, both of which are matters of record.
- **It does not resolve the funding figures that conflict.** Roughly $2.9 billion and roughly $3 billion in pledges are both in circulation from the same institution at different dates, and the $962 million disbursement figure has not been broken down by recipient.
- **It is written from outside the outbreak zone.** The most authoritative statements in it are the ones made by people in Beni, Butembo, Bunia and Mangina, quoted in the sources below.

## Part 15: How to use this

**If you fund this response.** Write financial reporting to the Congolese coordination structure into your grant conditions, with the same deadlines as your own reporting. Ring-fence frontline pay. Ask for the asset register. Ask what share of your money reached a Congolese organisation directly, and treat an unanswerable question as an answer.

**If you implement this response.** Publish your outbreak accounts this month without being asked. The reputational cost of being the first to do it is smaller than the cost of being among the two-thirds who did not attend the meeting.

**If you report on this response.** The useful question is not whether money is missing. It is which of the four explanations applies, organisation by organisation — and the second useful question is the one nobody has asked publicly: what are the current pay rates, and who sets them?

**If you live where this is happening.** Everything in Part 9 is a demand that can be made specifically, and most of it is already written into commitments the funders have signed.

**If you are arguing about this online.** The phrase is not a conspiracy theory, and the response is not a criminal enterprise. Both of those framings are comfortable, and both are wrong, and the distance between them is where the actual problem lives.
